Bayes' Rule

A belief is a shape over everything the world could be, and evidence is what pushes it around. Toss a coin and watch how much each toss is worth, in bits.

Somebody hands you a coin and asks how it is weighted. You have no idea, which is not the same as having no belief: it means your belief is spread thinly over every weighting the coin could have, rather than piled on one.

That is the whole move. A belief is not a number, it is a shape — a distribution over everything the world could be — and evidence does not replace it, it reweights it. Each toss asks every candidate bias the same question, how likely was that, and each candidate's share of your belief is multiplied by its own answer. Renormalize, and you are done. There is nothing else in Bayes' rule.

Three things below are worth watching for.

The first is that the shape both moves and narrows, and the narrowing is the part that matters. A belief that is merely centred in the right place is not knowledge; a belief that is centred in the right place and has nowhere else to go is. Watch the band the belief would bet on close in, and note how much slower it closes as it goes — four times the tosses to halve the width, the same square root that ran the last lesson.

The second is the price of being sure too early. Drag the faint curve up into a tall spike and start out certain of the wrong thing. The coin will still talk you round, because a likelihood of nearly zero is a heavy multiplier — but it will take a great many more tosses, and you can watch each one being spent. What each toss is worth is measured here in the currency of the first lesson: the divergence between the belief you held and the one you are now entitled to. Expected tosses teach almost nothing; the ones that surprise you are the ones that cost the world something to tell you.

There is a third thing, and it is the one worth arguing about. Turn on the second reader — someone who arrived convinced the coin favours tails, shown exactly the tosses you are shown — and watch two kinds of agreement arrive at very different speeds. What the two of you would bet converges quickly: a few hundred tosses and you are within a couple of hundredths of each other, still closing. What the two of you believe stays apart for far longer — the better part of a bit, at the point where your bets have all but merged — because the sharper a belief gets, the more a small displacement costs it in bits. Two people can agree on the answer while still holding genuinely different views, and the usual summary — a number and an interval — is exactly the part that converges first.

Weighted exactly down the middle. Starting from a flat belief, a hundred tosses will not make you certain of much — a coin near a half is the hardest kind to pin down, because heads and tails keep telling you opposite things.
