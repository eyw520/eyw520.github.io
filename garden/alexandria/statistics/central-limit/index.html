The Central Limit Theorem

Paint any distribution you like, add copies of it together, and watch the shape you drew disappear — leaving the one curve that survives every addition.

Add enough of anything together and you get the same curve.

That sentence should be alarming. The world is full of quantities built by adding: a day's takings, the height of a person, the error in a measurement, the position of a particle shoved about by its neighbours. They have nothing to do with one another, and their pieces have nothing to do with one another either — some are lopsided, some come in lumps, some are a coin toss. Yet the totals all end up wearing the same shape, and it is the shape you already know.

Below, the world is a row of bars, and you paint it. Make it lopsided, make it two spikes with a gap between them, make it a single rare event that almost never fires. Then add copies of it together.

Two things are worth watching, and they are different things. The sum spreads out — that is only arithmetic, the spread growing like the square root of the number of draws. Divide that growth out and what is left is the shape, and the shape is not arithmetic. It forgets what you drew.

The forgetting is measured here the way the theorem itself measures it: not by comparing heights, which a sum of whole numbers can never do fairly, but by the widest gap between the two cumulative curves. That number has a ceiling nobody can escape — Berry and Esseen's, drawn dashed — and it falls like one over the square root of n whatever you paint.

The poorest raw material there is: two outcomes, nothing in between. Eight tosses in and the gaps have filled; forty in and you would not know it started as a coin.

The mean and the spread survive. Everything else — the lean, the gaps, the second hump — is averaged out of existence, though not at the same speed: two spikes are all but gone by twenty draws, while a source that fires once in ten is still visibly lopsided at forty-eight. What you drew cannot change where this ends up. It only decides how long the journey takes, which is the third moment sitting in Berry and Esseen’s numerator — the one thing about your distribution the rate still remembers.
