Statistics

Maximum Entropy

Say what you know and nothing else, and the distribution picks itself. Say it three different ways and out fall the uniform, the exponential and the Gaussian.

Three distributions do most of the work in statistics, and they are usually introduced as three separate facts to be memorised. The uniform, when anything in a range is as good as anything else. The exponential, for waiting times and lifetimes and the gaps between rare events. The Gaussian, for everything else.

They are not three facts. They are one principle, asked three questions.

The principle is this: having said what you know, take the distribution that assumes the least on top of it — the one with the most entropy left in it, since entropy is exactly the amount of not-knowing a distribution admits to. Any other choice smuggles in a claim you were never entitled to make.

So say only that the quantity lives between two numbers, and the flattest thing on that range is the uniform. Add that you know its average, on a support with a floor and no ceiling, and the answer bends into the exponential — decaying, because putting mass further out would cost more average than you claimed. Add that you know its spread as well, and the Gaussian falls out, on its own, with nobody having chosen it.

Below, you make the claims and the distribution answers. The amber masses are a rival that says precisely the same things — same range, same average, same spread — while assuming a great deal more. It is not wrong; it is just less honest, and the bits it gives up are the divergence you met in the first lesson.

One piece of bookkeeping, since the numbers below are real rather than decorative. The axis is drawn as a couple of hundred equally spaced values, and every entropy quoted is the entropy over exactly those values — so is the shortfall beside it. Halve the spacing and both gain a bit, because a spread-out distribution gains somewhere to be while three spikes are still three spikes. What holds exactly at every spacing, and is the only thing being claimed, is the identity between them: the flattest answer's entropy is the rival's entropy plus the divergence from one to the other.

A quantity you know the limits of and nothing else — a proportion, a grade, a measurement with a floor and a ceiling. Watch the flat answer bend as you add each claim, and note that a Gaussian on a support this tight is a Gaussian with its tails cut off.
