The Determinant Is an Area

Not a formula with a mnemonic, and not a test for invertibility. It is the factor by which every area is multiplied — and the sign says whether the plane got turned over.

The determinant of a two by two matrix is introduced as ad minus bc, usually with a diagonal mnemonic, and it is left looking like a piece of arbitrary bookkeeping that happens to detect whether a matrix can be undone.

It is an area.

Specifically: take the unit square, push it through the matrix, and measure what comes out. That number is ad minus bc, and the formula is what the measurement looks like when you write it in coordinates.

Three things follow immediately, and they are the whole of what determinants are used for.

The first is that it is not about the square. Carry any shape you like through the matrix — the flag in the figure, a circle, anything — and its area is multiplied by the same number. A matrix does not have opinions about which regions to enlarge; it enlarges all of them equally.

The second is the sign. Push one arrow across the other and watch the number go through zero and come out negative, while the shape turns over. A negative determinant means the plane has been mirrored: the handedness of everything in it has been reversed, and the flag is the easiest way to see that happen since a square gives nothing away.

The third is what zero means. Zero is the instant the two arrows line up — the square is crushed onto a line, and every area in the plane becomes nothing at once. Nothing crushed flat can be brought back, since everything on that line came from a whole family of places and there is no way to say which. That is exactly what it means for the matrix to have no inverse. So "the determinant is zero" and "the matrix cannot be undone" are not two facts joined by a theorem. They are one fact, seen from two sides.

And it explains why determinants multiply. Do one matrix and then another, and areas are multiplied by the first factor and then by the second — so the determinant of the composition is the product of the determinants, which is otherwise a moderately tedious thing to verify in coordinates and here is barely worth saying out loud.

Zero is the moment the two arrows line up: the square is squashed flat onto a line, every area becomes nothing, and nothing can be undone — which is precisely the condition under which the matrix has no inverse. The determinant is not a test for invertibility that happens to be an area. It is an area, and that is why it is a test.
