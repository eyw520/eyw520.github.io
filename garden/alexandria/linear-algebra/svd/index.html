Linear Algebra

The Singular Value Decomposition

Every matrix ever written down is a turn, a stretch along two perpendicular directions, and another turn. Drag one into being and watch it come apart.

A matrix arrives as a box of numbers, which is a terrible way to meet one. Four numbers, no obvious relation between them, and a rule for multiplying that has to be memorised before it can be used. What the box does to the plane is nowhere on its face.

Here is what it does, and it is the same answer for every matrix that has ever been written down. It turns the plane. It stretches it along two perpendicular directions, each by its own amount. It turns it again.

That is all. Not a special case, not an approximation, not something that holds for the well-behaved ones — every matrix, including the ones that shear, the ones that reflect, and the ones that flatten the plane onto a line. Shear is not a fourth thing a matrix can do; it is what a turn and a stretch and a turn look like when you write them as one box.

The two amounts are the singular values, and almost everything you would want to know is read off them. Their product is the area scale. Their ratio says how badly the matrix favours one direction over another, which is what makes a problem ill-conditioned. And if you are forced to throw one away, the one you throw is the smaller, because what that costs is exactly its size — the cleanest optimality result in the subject, and the reason this decomposition sits underneath image compression, recommender systems and principal component analysis alike.

One note for anyone who has met this before. The usual statement keeps both stretches positive and allows the two turns to include a mirror. Here the turns are kept as turns and the mirror rides in the second stretch, which is allowed to go negative. It is the same decomposition either way; this arrangement is the one where every part of it is a move you could make with your hands.

Below, the circle is the plane before, the ellipse is the plane after, and the amber arrows are the matrix itself: drag them and you are writing it by hand. Then take it apart and watch the three moves go by one at a time.

The classic argument against the story: a shear slides the plane sideways and looks nothing like a turn and a stretch. Take it apart anyway. The two directions it stretches are not the axes — they sit at an angle, which is exactly why the shear looked like a fourth kind of thing.

There is nothing else a matrix can do.
