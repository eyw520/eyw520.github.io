The Directions a Matrix Leaves Alone

Most directions get turned. A few come back pointing exactly where they left, only longer or shorter — and those few tell you what the matrix is.

Point a vector somewhere and push it through a matrix. Almost always it comes out pointing somewhere else — turned, as well as stretched.

But swing the vector slowly round the circle and, for most matrices, you will pass through directions where the turning stops. The image points exactly along the original, only longer or shorter. Those directions are the eigenvectors, and how much longer or shorter is the eigenvalue.

That is the whole definition, and it is worth finding by hand before it is written down. Drag the dark arrow round the circle below and watch the angle between it and its image close to nothing, twice.

Why anyone cares takes one more step. If a matrix has two such directions, then in those directions it does nothing but stretch — so choose them as your axes and the matrix becomes a pair of numbers. Everything hard about it was an artefact of the coordinates it happened to be written in. That is what diagonalization is, and it is why eigenvectors turn up wherever a hard problem is being made easy: differential equations, vibration, quantum mechanics, the ranking in the previous course's chain.

The second figure plots the swing at every direction, so the eigenvectors are its crossings. It is the most direct way to see the third case, which is the one that makes this subject interesting.

Try the rotation. The curve never touches zero. No direction survives pointing where it started, because the matrix turns all of them — there is nothing to diagonalize and no amount of cleverness will find an axis that makes it a stretch. Such a matrix has eigenvalues, but they are complex numbers, and the imaginary part is precisely the turning you can see.

Then try the shear, which is the strange one. It has a repeated eigenvalue and only one direction to go with it, where it ought to have two. The curve grazes zero without crossing. A matrix like that cannot be diagonalized either, and the shortfall has a name — defective — which is as close as this subject gets to an insult.

A symmetric matrix, which is the well-behaved case: two invariant directions, at right angles to each other, one stretching more than the other. Swing the arrow round and the swing crosses zero twice.

A matrix with two of these is one you understand completely: pick those two directions as your axes and it is nothing but a pair of stretches. A matrix with none is turning the plane, and no choice of axes will ever make it look like anything else. The whole classification is decided by one sign — whether the trace squared beats four times the determinant — and you can watch it flip by dragging an amber arrow across the crossing.
