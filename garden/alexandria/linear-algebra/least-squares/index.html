Linear Algebra

The Error Is Perpendicular

Fitting a line is not a formula to memorise. It is projection: keep the part of the data a line can explain, and check that nothing a line could say is left in what remains.

Six points, not on a line. Draw the line that fits them best.

"Best" has to be defined before the sentence means anything, and the usual choice is: make the total of the squared vertical gaps as small as possible. The squares are drawn in the figure so that the definition is a thing you can see rather than a phrase you agree to.

Why squares, rather than the gaps themselves? Two reasons, and the second is the one that matters. The small reason is that squares are smooth, so the total has a single well-defined bottom, which the second figure shows as one clean valley. The large reason is that squaring turns the problem into geometry, and the geometry is the answer.

Here is the geometric statement. Think of the six measured values as one point in six-dimensional space — not six points on a plane, one point with six coordinates. The lines you are allowed to draw form a flat two-dimensional surface inside that space: all the things a line could possibly have produced. Your data is not on that surface, which is precisely what it means for the points not to lie on a line.

So drop a perpendicular. The closest point on the surface is the foot of the perpendicular from the data, and the line corresponding to that point is the fit. Everything left over — the residual — sticks out at right angles to the entire surface.

Which gives a test you can perform without solving anything, and it is in the readouts below. If the leftovers still summed to something, a constant shift would improve the fit and the line was not the best. If the leftovers still leaned on the input, a small tilt would improve the fit and the line was not the best. Both come back as zero, to the last decimal, at every configuration of the points you can drag them into. That is not a coincidence of the formula. It is the definition of having finished.

Drag one point far away and watch the line swing after it — squares punish distance twice over, so a single stray measurement has more say than any other point on the plot. Every criticism ever made of least squares starts there.

The two leftover readouts are the whole theory. Fitting a line means explaining as much of the data as a line can, and you know you have finished when nothing a line could say is left in the residue — the leftovers add to nothing, and they have no lean on the input either. In the language of the earlier lessons: the answer is the projection of the data onto the space of things a line can produce, and the error is what sticks out perpendicular to it. That is the only reason the awkward formula works, and it is the same picture whether you are fitting two coefficients or two thousand.
