Stochastic Processes

Where a Chain Settles

A process with no memory beyond where it stands, and yet it has a destination. Edit the steps and watch it forget where it began — or fail to.

A Markov chain is a process that has forgotten everything except where it is standing. Not where it has been, not how long it has been going, not what it did last time it was here. Only: I am here, and here is where I might go next.

That sounds like too little to say anything with, and it is the opposite. Almost every such process has a destination — not a place it stops, since it never stops moving, but a shape it settles into: a set of long-run proportions that stops changing even though the process does not. Start it anywhere you like and it ends up in the same shape, having forgotten which corner it started in.

Two things below are worth doing.

The first is to start the whole mass in one state and press the step button until nothing moves. Then start it somewhere else and watch it arrive at the same place. That destination is not the average of anything; it is the one distribution the chain leaves alone, and it can be solved for directly — which is what the pale rings behind the states are.

The second is to watch how it arrives, because it is more orderly than it has any right to be. The distance to the destination does not wander down. It is multiplied by the same number every step, and that number is a property of the chain rather than of where you started: the second eigenvalue, or as it is written here, the forgetting rate. Push a state's outgoing steps around with the bars and watch the rate move with them. Make a chain stubborn — everything staying where it is, nine times out of ten — and the rate climbs towards one, meaning almost nothing is forgotten per step; the process still arrives, but the half-life stretches out and out.

Then try the last preset, which is the reason this has any subtlety at all. It is a perfectly good chain, connected, with a perfectly good destination sitting there in the arithmetic. It never goes there. Every step moves the whole mass around the ring, so what you get is not a settling but a march — and no amount of running it changes that, because a chain with a beat has nothing to forget. The lesson is that having a destination and getting there are two different theorems, and the second one needs one more assumption than it looks like it should.

Tomorrow depends on today and on nothing before it. Sun tends to hold, rain tends to break — and after half a dozen steps, what you started with is gone. Whatever distance to the destination remains, this one throws away more than half of it every step.
