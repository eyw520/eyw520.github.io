Stochastic Processes

The Walk That Ends

A fair game, played to the finish, is decided by nothing but what you sat down with. Tilt it by one percentage point and watch the straight line bend.

Here is a process with one rule. Every turn you win one or lose one, and you stop when you have reached a target or run out.

There is no strategy in it and nothing to decide, and yet almost everything interesting about random processes with boundaries is already here — hitting probabilities, expected durations, martingales, and the reason a small edge is not a small thing.

Start with the fair version. Where does the chance of reaching the top come from? Not from anywhere clever: the chance of success from where you stand has to be the average of the chances from the two places you might be next, because one of them is where you will be. A quantity that equals its own average at every interior point, pinned at both ends, is a straight line. So on a fair game your chance of reaching twenty is exactly the fraction of twenty you brought — sit down with eight and you get there two times in five.

Which is a statement about fairness worth pausing on. The game does not care how you play or how long you take. Your expected finish is what you started with, and the only question is how it gets divided between the two ways of ending.

Now tilt it. The same argument still works, but a quantity whose average is weighted rather than even is no longer a line — it is a run of powers, and powers are ruthless. Move to forty-nine turns in a hundred and half a table stops being half a chance and becomes about an eighth. Double the size of the game and it drops again, to under a fiftieth. The bend in that curve is the entire business model of every casino that has ever operated: not winning often, but being slightly more likely to win each turn, and having enough turns.

The last number is the one nobody guesses right. How long does a fair game last, starting halfway to twenty? Ten times ten — a hundred turns, on average, from a game that only ever moves one step at a time. Fair games take a very long time to end, which is why they feel like they are going nowhere. They are.

At even odds the answer is the fraction of the table you brought, and nothing else about the game matters. Move the odds by a single point against you and the straight line sags into a curve — from half a chance down to about an eighth. The house does not need to win often. It needs only to be very slightly more likely to win each turn, and then to let the arithmetic of powers do the rest.
