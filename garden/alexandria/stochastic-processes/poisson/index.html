Waiting for Something With No Memory

Events that arrive with no schedule and no memory. However long you have already waited, the wait ahead of you has exactly the same distribution it had when you arrived.

Some things arrive without a schedule. Calls to a switchboard, decays in a lump of uranium, buses in a city that has given up, mutations along a strand of DNA. There is a rate — so many an hour, on average — and beyond that, nothing coordinating them.

Make that precise and you get the Poisson process, and it has three faces that turn out to be the same face.

The first is the timeline. Look at the events in the top figure and resist the urge to read anything into them. They clump. There are stretches with nothing in them and stretches with three in quick succession. That is not a pattern, it is the absence of one — genuinely unscheduled arrivals look far lumpier than most people expect, and a suspiciously even spacing would be evidence that something is coordinating them.

The second is the wait. The time until the next event is exponentially distributed, and the middle figure measures it. Then comes the property that makes this process what it is.

Say you have already been waiting a quarter of an hour. How much longer, on average? The same as when you arrived. Not less. Select an amount already waited, in the controls, and watch the distribution of the remaining wait fail to change — same shape, same average, exactly.

This is memorylessness, and it is genuinely strange. The bus is not "due". The atom is not "getting on". A component that fails this way is no more fragile after a year of service than on the day it was made. Only one distribution on the positive numbers has this property, and it is the exponential — which the statistics course arrived at from a completely different direction, as the flattest distribution with a given average. Both routes lead there because both are describing the same thing: a process about which nothing further is known.

The third face is the count. Fix a window of time and ask how many events land in it, and the answer is the Poisson distribution — whose spread is the square root of its mean, so a rate of nine an hour comes with a routine wobble of three. That is worth carrying around. A hospital averaging nine admissions an hour will see six one hour and twelve the next, and neither is a signal about anything.

The middle figure is the strange one. Select a wait you have already sat through and the distribution of what is left does not move — the same curve, the same average. A bus that arrives this way is no more due for having kept you waiting, and no component wearing out this way is any closer to failing than a new one. Only one distribution on the positive numbers behaves like that, and it is the exponential — which the third lesson of the statistics course produced from an entirely different direction, as the humblest distribution with a given average. The same object keeps turning up because it is what having no further information looks like.
