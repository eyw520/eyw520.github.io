Stochastic Processes

The Walk With No Smallest Step

Shrink the steps and take more of them, in the one proportion that leaves anything behind. What is left is continuous everywhere, straight nowhere, and infinitely long.

A pollen grain on water jerks about under the blows of molecules too small to see. The obvious way to model it is a random walk — a step this way, a step that way — but a real grain is not taking discrete steps, so the obvious model is wrong at every scale you look at.

So take the steps smaller and take more of them, and see what the walk becomes.

Everything depends on a proportion, and there is only one that works. Take n steps in a fixed unit of time and let each be of size one over the square root of n. Make them any larger and the paths fly off the page as you refine. Any smaller and they collapse onto the axis. That single scaling — the same square root as the central limit theorem, for the same reason — is the only one that leaves a picture behind.

And what it leaves is remarkable, which the first figure shows by not changing. Eight steps or four thousand, the bundle of paths looks the same: same spread, same character, same roughness. Only the drawing gets finer. That limit is Brownian motion, and its properties are worth stating because most of them are contradictory-sounding and all of them are true.

It is continuous: the path has no jumps. It has no slope anywhere: zoom in and it never straightens, exactly like the crinkled curve in the calculus course, except this one arrives by accident rather than by construction. Its length is infinite. The distance travelled up and down in the readouts grows without limit as you refine the steps, because a path that never straightens has to keep doubling back.

And then the one quantity that does not run away. Add up the squares of the steps rather than the steps themselves and the answer is one — the elapsed time — at every refinement, exactly. Squaring is the right power. Below it the sum runs away, above it the sum dies, and at exactly two it is the clock. That is why the calculus for these paths is written in terms of squared increments, and why it has an extra term in it that ordinary calculus does not.

The step size is not a free choice. Make the steps any larger than one over the root of their number and the paths fly off the page as you refine; any smaller and they flatten onto the axis. Exactly one scaling leaves a picture behind, and what it leaves is the same picture at every refinement — a path that is continuous everywhere, has no slope anywhere, travels an infinite distance in one unit of time, and whose squared travel is exactly that unit of time. That last quantity is the one thing that survives being looked at infinitely closely, which is why the calculus built for these paths is written in terms of it.
