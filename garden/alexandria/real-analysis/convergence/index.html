Settling Everywhere, and Settling at Once

Every one of these sequences settles at every single point. Only some of them settle as a whole, and everything you would want to conclude depends on which.

A sequence of functions converging looks like it should mean one thing. It means two, and the gap between them is where a century of careful mathematics lives.

The weak sense is the obvious one. Stand at a point, watch the values there as you go along the sequence, and see them settle. Do that at every point and the sequence converges pointwise. Nothing else is asked.

The strong sense asks for something that sounds almost the same and is not. Name a tolerance first — before anyone says where they are standing — and require one step number, past which the whole function is within that tolerance of the limit everywhere at the same time. That is uniform convergence, and the tube in the figure below is exactly the picture of it: the question is whether the curve eventually fits inside.

Every sequence here converges in the weak sense. Try to make each one fit in the tube.

The powers of x settle at zero everywhere below one, and at one exactly at the end. Yet however far along you go, there is a stretch just short of one where the function has not come down yet — and the stretch merely gets thinner, never disappearing. The tell is in the limit: each function in the sequence is perfectly continuous, and the limit has a jump in it. That cannot happen when the convergence is uniform. The jump is the failure, made visible.

The sliding bump is the cleanest case of the same thing. At every fixed point the bump eventually passes by and leaves you at zero forever. But at every step there is still a bump somewhere, exactly as tall as it ever was. The trouble does not shrink. It relocates.

The tall spike is the one worth arguing about. It converges to zero at every point, uniformly it does not, and — the part that catches people — the area under it stays at a half the whole way. So the limit of the integrals is a half and the integral of the limit is nothing. Swapping a limit and an integral is not a manipulation you are entitled to. It is a theorem, with conditions, and this is the example that shows the conditions are not decoration.

Each one continuous, and their limit is not: zero everywhere below one, and one at the end. Go as far along as the controls allow and there is always a stretch near the right edge that has not come down yet.

Pick any single place on the axis and every one of these sequences settles there — ask for a tolerance and a step number exists that meets it, at that place. The question that separates them is whether one step number can be found that works for every place at the same time. Where it can, the limit inherits the good behaviour of the things making it: continuity survives, integrals survive, derivatives sometimes survive. Where it cannot, all those guarantees are void, and the escaping bump shows you why — the trouble never goes away, it just moves.
