Real Analysis

Nowhere to Escape To

Drop infinitely many points into a closed interval and some place is crowded — always, whatever you do. Open one end and the same sequence slips out.

Scatter infinitely many points into the interval from nothing to one, however you like. Adversarially, if you prefer: try to spread them so that no place is crowded.

You cannot. Somewhere in that interval there is a point with infinitely many of your points arbitrarily close to it, and the argument is short enough to carry in your head.

Cut the interval in half. One of the halves still holds infinitely many points, because two finite piles cannot make an infinite one. Keep that half and cut it in half. Same argument, same conclusion. Do it forever and the brackets close on a single place, with infinitely many of your points in every neighbourhood of it, however small.

That is the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, and the figure below performs it. The hunt keeps whichever half holds more, and the count in the margin never runs out.

Notice what the argument needed, because it needed exactly two things and no more.

Bounded: without it the points can march off to infinity and never crowd anywhere. There is no halving to do.

Closed: this one is subtler and it is the whole reason the word compact exists. Take the sequence one, a half, a third, a quarter, and so on, living in the interval that excludes its left end. The points crowd around zero and nowhere else — and zero is not in the space. The hunt closes on a place that has been removed, so within the space itself there is no crowded point at all. Nothing is wrong with the sequence; the room has a hole in the floor.

This one theorem is quietly underneath most of the first course. It is why a continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains its maximum instead of merely approaching one. It is why continuity there is automatically uniform, which was the ε–δ lesson's punchline. And it is why so many theorems in analysis begin with the phrase "on a closed bounded interval" — not as bookkeeping, but because that is precisely the condition under which nothing can escape.

Odd terms climbing towards a fifth, even terms falling towards four fifths. The sequence itself converges to nothing at all, and it has two crowded places — the hunt is entitled to either, and finds one.

The hunt never fails on a closed bounded window, and the reason is almost too simple to feel like a proof: cut anything holding infinitely many terms in half, and one of the halves is still holding infinitely many. Repeat for ever and the brackets close on a place with terms arbitrarily near it, arbitrarily late in the sequence. That is Bolzano and Weierstrass, and it is why closed bounded intervals are where analysis prefers to work — it is the same reason continuity became uniform in the first lesson, and the same reason a continuous function on such an interval attains its maximum rather than merely approaching one.
