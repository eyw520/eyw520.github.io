Calculus

Zoom Until It Is Straight

A derivative is not a formula, it is a claim: look closely enough and this curve is a line. Close the window ten times over and see which curves keep the promise.

Take any curve you like and look at a smaller and smaller piece of it.

For almost anything you can draw, something quietly remarkable happens: the curve stops looking curved. Close the window by a factor of ten and the bend halves, then halves again, until what is on the screen is indistinguishable from a straight line. That line is the tangent, its slope is the derivative, and the whole of differential calculus is bookkeeping for finding that number without having to zoom in by hand.

It is worth doing by hand once, which is what the first figure is for. Watch the number for how bent it still looks: on a smooth curve it falls by a factor of ten every time you close the window by a factor of ten, and by the fourth or fifth step there is nothing left to see.

Then find out what the promise is worth.

The corner never straightens. Zoom in as far as the controls go and you get the same picture back — the same bend, at every magnification, because a corner is the same shape at every scale. The two side slopes disagree and go on disagreeing, and there is no single number that could be called the slope there.

The cusp fails differently and more violently. It has no corner; it has a vertical tangent, and the slopes do not disagree so much as run away to infinity together.

The wiggling one is the trap. It crosses zero infinitely often on its way to the origin and looks far worse behaved than the corner — and it is differentiable there, with slope zero. Being complicated and having no derivative are different things, which is worth knowing before meeting the last one.

The crinkled curve is Weierstrass's, and it is continuous everywhere and straight nowhere. Zoom in and it looks the same. Zoom in again and it looks the same. The slope of the chord never settles on anything: watch the second figure wander instead of levelling off. When it was published in 1872, the belief that a continuous curve must be smooth apart from a few awkward points had been treated as too obvious to prove for a century and a half. It was simply false, and this is the object that showed it.

Nothing special: a sine with a lean on it. Close the window step by step and watch the bend fall away by a factor of ten each time until the curve and the chord are the same line.

A derivative is not really a formula. It is the claim that this curve, looked at closely enough, is a straight line — and the slope of that line is the answer. Every rule you were made to memorise is a bookkeeping device for finding it without doing what you have just done by hand.
