The Curve That Is Its Own Slope

Every exponential grows in proportion to its size. Exactly one grows at precisely its size — and that base is not a choice anybody made.

Take any exponential curve — two to the x, say — and ask how steep it is.

The answer has a shape you might not expect. Its slope, at every point, is a fixed multiple of its own height there. Not roughly: exactly, and the same multiple at every point on the curve. That is what exponential growth is, stated as a differential equation rather than as a formula: the rate of increase is proportional to the amount present.

Which raises an obvious question. What is the multiple?

For two, it is about seven tenths — the curve grows at seventy percent of its height, so it is somewhat lazier than its own size. For ten, it is about two and a third — considerably more eager. Drag the base below and watch the amber slope curve slide underneath the green one, then over it.

So somewhere in between there is a base for which the multiple is exactly one, and there the curve is its own slope. Not proportional to it. Equal to it, as functions, everywhere. The second figure shows the crossing happening, and the number is about 2.71828.

Nobody picked it. It is not a convention like measuring angles in degrees or a choice of units — it is the solution of a question that had to have an answer, and there was only ever one.

The two remaining readouts are the same number arriving from directions that look unrelated.

Compound interest at a hundred percent, compounded twice, gives you 2.25. Twelve times a year, 2.613. Every day, 2.7146. Continuously, 2.71828, which is where it stops.

And the plain sum: one, plus one, plus a half, plus a sixth, plus a twenty-fourth — the reciprocal factorials, adding to 2.71828.

Three questions — the curve that is its own slope, the limit of compounding, the sum of the reciprocal factorials — with one answer. They are not three coincidences. They are three ways of asking what happens when growth is proportional to what is already there, and that is why this number turns up wherever anything grows, decays, cools, spreads or is paid interest on.

Every exponential grows in proportion to its own size — that is what makes it an exponential — and the constant of proportionality depends on the base. Doubling grows at about seven tenths of itself; ten grows at over twice itself. Somewhere between them is a base growing at exactly its own size, and there is only one. Nobody chose it. It is where compound interest lands when you compound continuously, and where one plus one plus a half plus a sixth is heading, and the reason all three are the same number is that they are all asking this one question.
