A Function From One Point

Everything a curve does at a single place — its height, slope, bend and the rest — is often enough to rebuild it everywhere. Often, and not always, and the exception is the interesting part.

Here is an outrageous proposal. Stand at one point of a curve and write down everything it is doing there: its height, its slope, how fast the slope is changing, how fast that is changing, and so on for ever. Claim that this is enough to rebuild the entire curve, somewhere else, where you never looked.

For a great many functions the proposal is exactly right, and the polynomial that does it is Taylor's. Each term matches one more derivative at the centre, and each one buys a little more agreement further out.

Watch it happen below. One term is a horizontal line at the right height. Two is the tangent, which is the previous lesson's answer. Three adds the bend. By eight or thirteen terms the polynomial and the sine are indistinguishable across the whole window, and you can push the centre around and watch the match travel with it.

Now the trouble.

One over one plus x squared is a perfectly ordinary function. It is smooth everywhere, it has no corners, it never blows up, and at x equal to two it is doing nothing remarkable whatever — it equals a fifth and carries gently on. Its Taylor series about zero is completely useless there. Not slightly worse: the partial sums swing further apart with every term you add, so more terms make the answer worse, without limit and for ever.

The shaded band in the figure is where the series works. It ends at exactly one, and nothing about the function marks that place.

The resolution is one of the moments where mathematics quietly rearranges itself. Nothing on the real line explains the wall — so the explanation is not on the real line. Extend the function to complex numbers and it has a genuine blow-up at the square root of minus one, which sits at distance exactly one from the centre. A power series converges in a disc, and the disc can only grow until it hits a singularity, wherever that singularity happens to live. The series about zero cannot reach two because it cannot get past i, which is a place the series was never asked about and can nevertheless feel.

The encouraging case. Add terms and the polynomial reaches further out in both directions, and it never stops improving — drag the dark point and watch the whole approximation pick itself up and move with the centre.

A Taylor series is built from what the function is doing at a single point — its value, its slope, its bend, and so on — and yet it can reproduce the function some distance away. Some functions let it work everywhere. Others have a wall, and the strangest thing in this lesson is that the function gives no sign of it: one over one plus x squared is as smooth and finite at two as it is at nothing, and its series about zero is worthless there. The explanation is not on this line at all. It is that the function blows up at the square root of minus one, exactly one unit from the centre — and the series can feel a singularity it can never reach.
