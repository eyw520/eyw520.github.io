Calculus

The Fundamental Theorem

Area gathers exactly as fast as the curve is high. Sweep it yourself and watch the two numbers stay equal, on any function you like.

Calculus is two questions, and for a long time they were two subjects.

How steep is this curve here? That one is about a single point, and it is answered by zooming in until the curve looks straight.

How much is underneath it? That one is about a whole stretch, and it is answered by chopping the stretch up and adding.

Nothing about those questions suggests they have anything to do with each other. One is local and about slopes; the other is global and about sums. The fundamental theorem says they are the same question asked from opposite ends: differentiating undoes accumulating, exactly, always.

Here is the version to hold on to, and the figures below are nothing but this sentence drawn. Sweep out the area under a curve, keeping a running total as you go. That total is itself a function of how far you have swept. And the rate at which the total grows — its slope — is the height of the curve you are sweeping under.

Which is obvious once you see it, and it is worth pausing to make sure you have. Where the curve is high, you are adding a lot of area per step, so the total climbs steeply. Where the curve is low, you are adding almost nothing, so the total flattens. Where the curve crosses below the axis you start subtracting, and the total turns over and comes back down. Where the curve touches zero, the total is momentarily not moving at all — which is why the peaks of the second figure sit exactly above the crossings of the first.

The two numbers under the figures are the point. The left one is the height of the function, read off the top figure. The right one is the slope of the area curve, measured on the bottom figure by looking at that curve alone — the calculation never consults the function. Drag the handle anywhere on any of these shapes and they stay equal.

Then drag the amber handle, which moves where the gathering starts. The lower curve slides bodily up or down and does not otherwise change: the area from a different starting point differs by a constant, and a constant has no slope. That is the whole content of the "+ C" that gets tacked onto every integral, and it is easier to watch than to be told.

One arch of a sine. The total climbs fastest at the top of the arch, flattens off as the curve comes down, and ends at exactly two — which is the answer the calculus gives for this area, and the figure never used it.

The first two numbers are the same number, wherever you put the handle, on any function you pick. That is the fundamental theorem: differentiating undoes accumulating. Drag the amber handle and the whole lower curve slides up or down without changing shape — moving where you start changes the area by a constant and changes its slope not at all, which is the constant of integration, drawn rather than apologised for.
