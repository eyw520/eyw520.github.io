Measure Theory

Almost Every Point Is an Inside Point

Stand inside a set and close the window. For nearly every place you could be standing, what you see fills up — even in a set that contains no interval at all.

Stand at a point of a set and look at a small window around yourself. How much of what you can see is set?

For an ordinary interval the answer is obvious. Stand anywhere inside it and the window is entirely full, at every scale; stand outside and it is entirely empty; stand exactly on the edge and it is half full for ever, which is the one interesting place.

Now do it in a set with no edges anywhere.

The moth-eaten set from the first lesson contains no interval, however short. Between any two of its points there is a hole, and there are holes at every scale, and it looks in the top figure like a scattering rather than a solid. Stand at one of its points and close the window, and the fraction that is set climbs to above ninety-nine percent.

Both of these things are true at once, and neither is a trick. The set is riddled with holes at every scale, and the holes near any given point of it are so much smaller than the distance to them that they take up almost nothing of a sufficiently small window.

That is Lebesgue's density theorem, and the statement is the surprising bit: for any measurable set at all, almost every one of its points is like this. Not the nice ones, not the ones away from the edges — almost all of them, where "almost all" means the exceptions have measure zero. A measurable set is, in this precise sense, made almost entirely of inside points, no matter how ragged it looks.

Two things to try.

Put the marker in a hole and close the window: the fraction goes to nothing just as decisively. Points off the set are almost all outside points, by the same theorem.

Then try the dust — the middle-thirds set, which has no length at all. Stand on one of its points and close the window as far as the controls go, and the fraction does not climb. A set of measure zero has no inside points to be made of, and the theorem says nothing about it because there is nothing to say.

The case where everything behaves. Inside, the window fills completely at any scale; outside it is empty; and at the single edge point it sits at exactly half for ever — one exceptional point out of a whole interval, which is what a set of measure zero looks like.

The moth-eaten set is the one worth standing inside. It contains no interval — there is a hole between any two of its points, at every scale — and yet from almost any of its own points, a small enough window is more than ninety-nine percent full of it. Sparse in the sense of containing nothing solid, and dense in the sense of filling what surrounds it. That is Lebesgue's density theorem, and it says almost every point of any measurable set is a place like the one you are standing in.
