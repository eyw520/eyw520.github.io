Measure Theory

Slice the Range, Not the Domain

One change of question separates the integral everyone is taught from the one everyone uses. Here is a function that shows you the difference, and it is not a nasty one.

The integral you were taught cuts the ground under a curve into thin vertical strips and adds them up. It is such an obvious thing to do that it takes an effort to notice a choice was made at all.

Here is the choice. To measure the strip over a slice of the domain you have to know how high the function gets there — and if the function is doing something complicated on that slice, you are stuck with a range of answers rather than one. The usual repair is to take two: a box that certainly contains the strip and a box that certainly fits inside it. Refine the slices, and if those two numbers close on each other, the function has an integral, and that number is it.

Almost everything closes. Continuous functions close, monotone ones close, anything with a few jumps closes. Two centuries of mathematics ran on the assumption that the ones that did not close were pathological — deliberately constructed monsters, safely ignorable.

They are not. Below is a set built by taking the middle quarter out of an interval, then the middle sixteenth out of each of the two pieces left, then the middle sixty-fourth out of each of those four, and so on forever. What survives contains no interval at all: pick any two points of it, however close, and there is a hole between them. And yet it still weighs half of what it started with — the removed pieces add up to only a half.

Now integrate its indicator: the function that is one on the set and zero off it. Every box drawn from above catches a piece of the set, so it counts the full height. No box from below fits inside the set, because the set contains no interval, so every one of them counts nothing. However finely you slice, the two numbers never come closer than a half apart, and the function has no Riemann integral — not because it is pathological, but because the question was asked in the wrong direction.

Lebesgue's change is the whole subject in one sentence. Do not ask how high the function gets on a piece of the domain. Ask how big the set is where the function takes a given value. The moth-eaten set has a size — a half — and the moment you are allowed to say so, the integral is a half and there was never any difficulty.

What the second figure shows is that this is not a lucky escape. Slicing the range, the two bounds are one band apart by construction, whatever function you point at, because the only thing being measured is the size of a set. The difficulty was never in the function. It was in the question.

The easy case, and the reason nobody worries. Sixteen slices already box the answer to within a few hundredths, and both ways of cutting agree to as many decimals as you care to read.
