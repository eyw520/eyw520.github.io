Measure Theory

Hiding the Rationals

There is a rational number between any two numbers you can name. Cover every single one of them with a thousandth of an inch of cloth, and watch it work.

Name two numbers, as close together as you like. There is a fraction between them. Name a stretch of the line, however short. It holds infinitely many fractions.

The rationals are everywhere. There is nowhere on the line you can stand without one being arbitrarily close, and no gap between them anywhere. Any sensible person would conclude that they take up most of the room.

They take up none of it.

Here is the whole argument, and you can do it with the controls below. The rationals can be put in a list — first, second, third, and so on, missing none. That is the surprising part, and it is why they are called countable: fractions are pairs of whole numbers, and pairs of whole numbers can be marched through in order.

Now name a budget. Anything you like: a tenth, a thousandth. Give the first rational on the list an interval of half your budget. Give the second an interval of a quarter. The third an eighth, and so on down. Every rational has been covered by an interval. Not most of them, not all but a few — every one. And the intervals add up to your budget, because halves and quarters and eighths add up to one.

Since the budget was yours to choose and can be made as small as you please, the rationals must have size zero. Dense as they are, they take up no room at all. Everything the line is made of, in the sense of length, is irrational.

Two things are worth doing below.

Drag the budget down and watch the covering thin out to nothing while still catching every rational you can point at — because the covering does not need to be big, it needs to be clever about where it spends.

Then drag the marker onto a number and see whether it is hidden. Fractions are always caught. Try the square root of two less one, which is where the marker starts: as you take more of the list and less of the budget, it sits out in the open, uncovered, along with almost the whole of the line.

Between any two numbers you can name, however close, there is a rational — so the covered set reaches into every stretch of the line there is. And you have just hidden all of it under a thousandth of a unit of cloth. Being everywhere and taking up room are different properties, and the only reason this works is that the rationals can be put in a list. The irrationals cannot, which is exactly why no budget covers them.
